Tarantino writes ensembles like jazz charts: everyone gets a solo, and the movie lives or dies on whether those solos still feel sharp decades later. This isn't a ranking of films by Rotten Tomatoes. It's a walk through three casts using ActorRating's performance pages — the unit the site actually measures.

Start with *Pulp Fiction*, still the template. Then *Django*, where the dialogue weaponizes history. Then *Hollywood*, where the ensemble is about faded stardom and sudden violence. Score the people, not the poster.

## John Travolta in Pulp Fiction

Vincent Vega is cool as a lifestyle and incompetent as a professional — Travolta plays both without apology. The dance floor is iconic; the accidental shooting is the character. Soft charisma with a gun is still one of Tarantino's best contradictions.

## Samuel L. Jackson in Pulp Fiction

Jules is the ensemble's moral lightning rod. Jackson makes scripture sound like a threat and a punchline at the same time. If *Pulp Fiction* has a gravitational center, it's him — supporting on paper, foundational in practice.

## Uma Thurman in Pulp Fiction

Mia Wallace is a myth of cool that Thurman keeps human enough to bleed. The overdose sequence is the film dropping its sunglasses. She has to go from queen of the Jack Rabbit Slim's booth to someone fighting for breath — and she does it without losing the character's strange dignity.

## Jamie Foxx in Django Unchained

Django's arc is the film's spine: enslaved man to hunter to legend. Foxx plays the learning curve without soft-pedaling the rage. In a movie full of verbal fireworks, he often wins by listening — then acting.

## Christoph Waltz in Django Unchained

Dr. King Schultz is Tarantino's favorite kind of European: polite, verbose, and suddenly lethal. Waltz makes charm a tactic. He's the mentor figure who never quite becomes soft, which is why the partnership with Django stays sharp instead of sentimental.

## Leonardo DiCaprio in Django Unchained

Calvin Candie is hospitality as sadism. DiCaprio leans into the smile until it curdles — a supporting antagonist who eats scenes by offering drinks. It's grotesque on purpose, and the performance knows exactly how long to hold the room hostage.

## Brad Pitt in Once Upon a Time in Hollywood

Cliff Booth is competence as mystique — the stuntman who might be a killer and definitely knows how to fix a rooftop antenna. Pitt underplays until the finale asks him not to. The ensemble needs that calm; Hollywood's anxiety sits on his shoulders.

## Leonardo DiCaprio in Once Upon a Time in Hollywood

Rick Dalton is vanity, panic, and surprising sweetness. DiCaprio makes a fading TV cowboy pathetic without making him small. It's a lead performance that still functions as part of an ensemble machine — he needs Pitt and Robbie to complete the joke and the ache.

## Margot Robbie in Once Upon a Time in Hollywood

Sharon Tate barely speaks, and Robbie still builds a full presence out of movement, light, and the decision not to turn her into a tragedy prop before the movie earns it. In an ensemble about men spinning out, she's the life the film is trying — awkwardly, sincerely — to protect.
