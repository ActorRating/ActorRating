Christopher Nolan casts like a director who expects the second and third chairs to do real work. The internet usually collapses those films into one lead and one villain. ActorRating is for the rest of the frame — the district attorney, the father figure, the scientist, the spouse — the performances that make the set pieces mean something.

## Aaron Eckhart in The Dark Knight

Harvey Dent gets half a movie as hope and half as tragedy, and Eckhart never plays either as a costume change. The Two-Face turn only lands because the upright prosecutor was already slightly too sharp, slightly too hungry. In a film famous for Ledger, Eckhart is the moral geometry: what Gotham loses when it breaks its white knight.

## Michael Caine in Inception

Caine's Miles barely gets screentime, which is the point. He walks into *Inception* as the only adult who knows Cobb is lying to himself, and he does it without a speech about dreams. Nolan uses Caine the way some directors use score — as tonal authority. Underrated because the film trains you to chase the kick, not the quiet no.

## Cillian Murphy in The Dark Knight

Scarecrow is a leftover from *Begins* and somehow still the scariest sober person in the room. Murphy plays Crane like a man who enjoys bureaucracy as much as fear gas — small, precise, almost amused. It's a supporting turn that refuses comic-book volume, which is why it still cuts through the chaos every rewatch.

## Anne Hathaway in Interstellar

Brand is the film's argumentative spine: love as data, duty as cruelty, hope as method. Hathaway has to sell philosophy inside a blockbuster without sounding like a TED Talk. She mostly wins. When *Interstellar* works emotionally, it's often because she refuses to let Cooper's grief be the only valid science.

## Jessica Chastain in Interstellar

Adult Murph is the terrestrial half of the epic — rage, intellect, and a lifetime of being the child left behind. Chastain plays the "solve gravity" plot as personal unfinished business. She's not waiting for a miracle; she's furious that the universe asked her to. That anger is what makes the bookshelf ending feel earned instead of cute.

## Robert Downey Jr. in Oppenheimer

Lewis Strauss is the film's second engine: smiling patronage that curdles into procedural revenge. Downey Jr. dials the charm into a weapon and then lets you watch it misfire. In a three-hour biopic about genius and guilt, he's the reminder that institutions have egos too — and they keep score longer than scientists do.

## Emily Blunt in Oppenheimer

Kitty Oppenheimer could have been "worried wife" wallpaper. Blunt plays her as the only person in the room who will say the quiet part — loyalty, resentment, and alcohol included. The security hearing scenes are half Strauss and half Kitty's refusal to perform grace. That's supporting work that changes the temperature of the whole film.
