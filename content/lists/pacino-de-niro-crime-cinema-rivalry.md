For fifty years, Pacino and De Niro have been treated like a single myth with two faces. ActorRating is a better tool than the myth: same five dimensions, different roles, no obligation to declare a winner before you've scored the work.

This list walks the crime lane specifically — the Corleones, Tony Montana, and the *Heat* duel — because that's where the rivalry is most often argued in bars and comment sections.

## Al Pacino in The Godfather

Michael Corleone begins as the war-hero outsider and ends as the man who closes the door. Pacino's early Michael is quiet on purpose — the performance is a slow removal of oxygen from the room. If you only know late-career Pacino volume, start here. The restraint is the violence.

## Al Pacino in The Godfather Part II

Part II is Michael without illusions. Pacino hardens the voice, empties the eyes, and turns family into strategy. It's still one of the coldest lead performances in American film — and on ActorRating, it's the kind of turn community voters recognize as craft, not just legacy.

## Robert De Niro in The Godfather Part II

Young Vito is the counter-myth: immigrant grace, street calculation, a softer register than Brando's godfather without losing authority. De Niro builds power as manners. Put him next to Pacino's Michael and you get the film's real thesis — two generations, two temperatures, one crime family.

## Al Pacino in Scarface

Tony Montana is appetite with an accent and a mountain of coke. Pacino goes big because the character is big — and the dare is whether you can feel the tragedy under the excess. Plenty of people hate the movie's bombast; fewer deny that the performance *is* the bombast. Rate it as controlled chaos or reject it — either way, score the turn.

## Al Pacino in Heat

Vincent Hanna is the Pacino of the '90s: wired, talkative, alive to the hunt. He's not Michael anymore. He's a man who needs the chase to feel real. In *Heat*, that energy is the point — a detective whose personal life is collateral damage.

## Robert De Niro in Heat

Neil McCauley is the clean professional: few words, exact plans, a code that will kill him. De Niro plays stillness as dominance. The diner scene only works because both men believe they're the adult in the room. Score them separately. Then admit the rivalry is better as a double feature than a verdict.
