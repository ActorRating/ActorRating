Awards season loves a supporting category because it's where movies hide their sharpest knives. These six turns come from cohort films already on ActorRating — no padding, no Parasite placeholders waiting on cast ingest. Score them as performances that had to share the poster and still changed the temperature.

## Robert Downey Jr. in Oppenheimer

Strauss is patronage with a long memory. Downey Jr. spends half the film as a helpful peer and half as a man rewriting the record. Supporting on the call sheet; structural in the edit. If you only remember the Trinity test, go back for the hearing — that's where this performance sharpens.

## Emily Blunt in Oppenheimer

Kitty refuses to be the biopic spouse who waits in doorways. Blunt plays loyalty as a contact sport: sharp, tired, protective, and occasionally scorched. In a movie about men and machines, she's the reminder that the private cost had a face and a drinking problem.

## America Ferrera in Barbie

Gloria's parking-lot monologue is the film saying the quiet part with a straight face. Ferrera has to land sincerity inside a neon comedy without becoming a pamphlet. She does. It's supporting work that gives the movie its argument — and gives ActorRating a clean example of a performance audiences talk about separately from "the Barbie discourse."

## Ke Huy Quan in Everything Everywhere All at Once

Waymond is kindness as strategy and sadness as history. Quan makes the goofy dad, the warrior, and the heartbroken husband feel like one person under extreme pressure. Supporting category or not, this is the emotional engine of the multiverse — the performance that makes the absurdity hurt.

## Jamie Lee Curtis in Everything Everywhere All at Once

Deirdre is IRS villainy that mutates into something stranger and funnier. Curtis commits to the hair, the rage, and the eventual crack in the armor. She's proof that "supporting" can still mean scene ownership — especially when the scene is a tax office fight that becomes cosmic.

## Robert De Niro in Killers of the Flower Moon

William Hale smiles like a neighbor and hunts like a system. De Niro plays American evil as hospitality — soft voice, hard ledger. In a film that could have been only about DiCaprio's emptiness, Hale is the architecture. Rate the stillness. The menace is in how little he needs to raise his voice.
