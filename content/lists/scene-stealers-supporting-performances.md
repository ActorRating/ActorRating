A "supporting" credit is a billing decision, not a wattage limit. Some performances walk into someone else's movie and rearrange the furniture. ActorRating is useful here because you can score the stealer and the lead on the same scale — and see whether the community agrees about who owned the room.

## Samuel L. Jackson in Pulp Fiction

Jules Winnfield is the reason people still misquote Ezekiel. Jackson takes Tarantino's dialogue — already a weapon — and aims it like a sermon that might end in a shooting. Travolta gets the dance; Jackson gets the conversion. If you've ever left *Pulp Fiction* talking about the briefcase *and* the diner, that's Jules doing supporting work that behaves like a co-lead.

## Brad Pitt in Fight Club

Officially, Tyler Durden is the supporting chaos agent. Practically, Pitt is the film's id with a six-pack and a philosophy pamphlet. The movie needs Norton's unnamed narrator to be porous enough for Tyler to walk in and take over — which is another way of saying Pitt's job is to be unforgettable. Rate Tyler as a performance, not as a poster.

## Edward Norton in Fight Club

Norton is here as the necessary counterweight. Scene-stealing only matters if there's a scene to steal. His narrator is the soft clay: repressed, funny, increasingly terrified of his own freedom. On ActorRating, Norton's Community Rating has already shown up hot — a reminder that "lead" and "stealer" can trade energy depending on the night you watch.

## Jonah Hill in The Wolf of Wall Street

Donnie Azoff is the gremlin on Jordan Belfort's shoulder — greed without the salesman's polish. Hill plays him loud, sticky, and weirdly loyal, the friend who makes every bad idea worse and somehow more honest. In a movie designed as a DiCaprio showcase, Donnie is the supporting performance that keeps the carnival from becoming a monologue.

## Leonardo DiCaprio in The Wolf of Wall Street

The lead, included on purpose. DiCaprio's Belfort is a three-hour high-wire act of charm and rot. Put his score next to Hill's and you get the useful question: did the supporting player punch up into the lead's altitude, or did the lead leave enough oxygen for someone else to burn? That's the comparison this site is for.
